PART 2 · THE MAKING

Presenter B

Slides 3 and 4

Slide 3 · How the models learnabout 1:45

Thank you, [name A]. I'll explain the AI techniques we used.

The first one is supervised learning. The model learns from examples where we already know the right answer. For the sea creature detector, we used 7,637 photos from Open Images and the Aquarium Combined dataset, with every creature marked by a box. For the sound detective, we used 13,825 sound clips from FSD50K and Freesound, each labelled with the sounds it contains.

The second technique is transfer learning. We didn't train from zero. We started from models that were already trained on huge datasets, and fine-tuned them on our data. For photos, that's YOLO26 Nano, a small object-detection network. For sound, that's EfficientAT, which was trained on AudioSet.

Third, we split the data fairly into training, validation and test sets. The test set stays frozen: 767 photos and 4,216 sound clips that the models never see during training. So our scores come from data the models have never seen.

And finally, sound is multi-label. We turn the audio into a spectrogram, which is basically a picture of sound. The model looks at one-second slices, every half second, and one slice can contain several sounds at the same time.

If there's time, point at the banner: the photos move through Collect, Train, Test and Ship, and at Test each one gets the box our real model gave it.

SPACE
Slide 4 · How they predict, and how we measureabout 1:45

Next: how the models make predictions, and how we measure them.

The sea model does object detection. It draws a box around each creature and gives a confidence score. It often draws several boxes on the same animal, so we use IoU, which measures how much two boxes overlap, and non-max suppression, which keeps only the best box.

Then we use thresholds. A creature is shown only if the model is at least 45% sure. The sound model has a separate threshold for each of its 17 sounds. We tuned those on the validation set, never on the test set, so the test score stays honest.

To measure the models, we use mAP, mean average precision. For the detector we use mAP at 50, which means a box counts as correct if it overlaps the real box by at least half. For sound we also use F1. And for each model we keep a list of how it fails.

Last, both models are exported to ONNX and run with ONNX Runtime Web inside the browser. That's why nothing gets uploaded.

PART 3 · THE MODELS

Presenter C

Slides 5 and 6

Slide 5 · Sea creature detectorabout 1:25

Thank you, [name B]. This part of the proposal asks for the expected result as a UI prototype. But as you can see, the label just changed from "expected" to "built". This is already working, and the video on the right is a real screen capture of the live page.

This is our first model, the sea creature detector. You give it a photo, and it finds and boxes 14 kinds of sea creatures: fish, sharks, dolphins, sea turtles, penguins and more.

On 767 test photos it never saw, it scores 0.74 mAP at 50. On aquarium photos, it does even better: 0.82.

And here's the honest part. It mixes up seals and sea lions, and dolphins and whales, and it misses small fish that are far away. You can see real mistakes in the video: it calls a seal a sea lion, it calls a shark a plain fish, and in the school of fish it finds only 9 of the 23 fish.

Slide 6 · Sound detectiveabout 1:20

Our second model is the sound detective. It listens for 17 everyday sounds, like a dog barking, a siren, glass breaking or knocking, and shows when each one happens on a timeline. You can record, upload a file, or let it listen live.

The video shows its real output for one of our samples, called "Two sounds at once". You can see it hearing speech, traffic and clapping at the same time, each with its confidence.

On 4,216 test clips, it scores 0.79 mean average precision. We also made a design choice here: a bigger version scores 0.79 at 20.5 megabytes, and a smaller one scores 0.76 at 6.5 megabytes. We chose the more accurate one.

Its weak spots: the doorbell is its hardest sound, footsteps and car horns are also difficult, and the timing is still rough. It finds about half of the sounds within half a second of when they really start.

PART 4 · WHAT'S NEXT

Presenter D

Slides 7 and 8

Slide 7 · RAG assistantsabout 1:10

Thank you, [name C]. Besides the two models, we're also building something bigger: RAG assistants.

RAG means retrieval-augmented generation. When you ask a question, the assistant first searches its own documents for the passages that answer it. Then a small language model writes the reply using only those passages. So it answers from its documents, not from guessing.

Like everything else in the lab, it runs fully in the browser. It uses Qwen 2.5, a 1.5-billion-parameter language model, plus a small search model, on WebLLM.

We've built two assistants: one for a university, the University of Raffel Luo, and one for a district emergency office, Pathum Rai District.

We've tested them, but the work isn't complete yet. Right now we're measuring how well different defences protect them against tricky prompts, so we don't have final results to share today.
